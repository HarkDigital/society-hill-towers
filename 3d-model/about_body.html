Three identical apartment towers rise from a shared plaza two blocks off the Delaware — the centerpiece of the Washington Square East urban renewal plan, won in competition by developer Webb & Knapp with a young I. M. Pei as architect. Completed in 1964, they put a modernist exclamation point on a neighborhood of 18th-century rowhouses, and the pairing still defines Society Hill's skyline.

The facade is the structure: a load-bearing egg-crate of cast-in-place concrete on a six-foot module, with floor-to-ceiling aluminum-framed windows set deep into the grid. At the base the grid lands on a colonnade of slender columns around a recessed glass lobby. On the plaza stand Leonard Baskin's bronze Old Man, Young Man, The Future (1966) and, to the west, 37 flat-roofed brick townhouses Pei designed as the towers' low-rise counterpoint.

Every other building — about 2,800 in detail around the towers, and hundreds of thousands more across the whole city from the Northeast to the airport — is extruded from its real OpenStreetMap footprint at its LiDAR-measured height: roughly a quarter-million heights come from Philadelphia's 2022 aerial LiDAR survey (City of Philadelphia building footprints; USGS/NOAA point cloud), and in the Society Hill core the roof forms themselves — gable, hip, ridge direction, eave and ridge heights — are measured from the same flight. Facades draw on the city's property records (OPA): each building's construction era, material, storefront use and story count pick its brick, siding, schist or curtain-wall dress and its true window rows, and every roof wears the color sampled from the city's 2024 aerial orthophotos. The blocks you walk (and fly over) are the blocks that are there.
